Rubric · Problem understanding (5)

Industry need

Car makers run thousands of virtual crash tests (FMVSS 208, Euro NCAP) before building a prototype. A full model has millions of elements and tens of thousands of time steps, so a single run can take hours to days on one core.

Scope

A simplified explicit crash workload: spring-mass vehicle lattice with elastoplastic buckling, rigid barrier contact and self-contact, frontal impact at 56 km/h, partitioned across CPU cores with OpenMP.

Computational bottleneck

Per-step stage time at 1 thread, 100k mesh, from the assembly sweep. Spring forces and force assembly dominate. They are memory-bound (arithmetic intensity 0.38 FLOP/byte), so bandwidth limits the speedup.

Problem statement

Given a frontal 56 km/h impact against a rigid wall, compute node trajectories, wall force, intrusion and the energy budget for every 2.5 µs step, then cut wall-clock time by decomposing the per-step workload across CPU cores without changing the physics.

Objectives

  1. Build a correct explicit solver whose energy closes to under 0.1 %.
  2. Profile it and find the stage that dominates each step.
  3. Decompose the mesh and the force assembly across OpenMP threads, with no data races.
  4. Measure speedup, efficiency and the Karp–Flatt serial fraction at two mesh sizes.
  5. Explain the limits (memory bandwidth, hybrid cores) and recommend a scale-out architecture.

Workload size

Why a single core is not enough

Stakeholders

  • CAE engineers: need overnight design-of-experiments sweeps across dozens of variants.
  • Safety and homologation teams: need repeatable, energy-checked runs for FMVSS 208 and Euro NCAP evidence.
  • IT / HPC operations: need to size cores, memory bandwidth and licences.

Constraints and assumptions

  • Spring-mass lattice with elastoplastic springs, not shell or solid finite elements.
  • Rigid, frictionless barrier; penalty contact and spatial-hash self-contact.
  • A single shared-memory laptop: no cluster and no GPU in the measured runs.
  • FP64 by default. FP32 is benchmarked as a separate assembly mode.

Test hardware

Explain: the crash step loop is repeated tens of thousands of times. Each step is dominated by force computation, which is what we parallelise.

ASSEMBLY STRATEGY (selected mesh)
WHY THIS DESIGN
  • Shared-memory OpenMP: one laptop with 12 vCPUs and no cluster. Threads share the mesh, so no halo copies are needed.
  • Spatial domain decomposition: nodes are split along the vehicle length, so each thread owns a contiguous slab and keeps cache locality.
  • Race-free assembly: the node-centric CSR gather reads its neighbours and writes only its own node. No atomics and no private copies.
  • Barriers separate S1 to S5. The global min time step (S5) is a reduction.

SYNCHRONISATION POINTS
  • 4 implicit barriers per step (end of S1, S2, S3 and S4) plus a reduction(min:dt) in S5.
  • Over 45,000 steps that is about 180,000 barriers, so barrier cost is the floor on the time per step.
  • No critical sections and no locks in the hot path.

#pragma omp parallel num_threads(p)
{
  #pragma omp for schedule(static) nowait
  for(s=0;s<S;++s) spring_force(s);
  #pragma omp barrier
  #pragma omp for schedule(static)
  for(i=0;i<N;++i) F[i]=gather(csr,i);
  #pragma omp for reduction(min:dt)
  for(i=0;i<N;++i) dt=min(dt,cfl(i));
}

    • Cost: S(p) flattens after 8 threads, so extra cores on one socket waste money. Scale out across nodes and use GPUs for the memory-bound kernels.
